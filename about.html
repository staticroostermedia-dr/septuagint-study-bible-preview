About the Septuagint Study Bible

The Greek Septuagint (Rahlfs 1935), with every word tappable. Built as a study aid for beginners: the word card shows the word, its dictionary form and grammar chips (tap a chip for a one-line explanation); meanings from two dictionaries (Abbott-Smith and LSJ tabs); a compact forms table (forms found in the LXX, current one highlighted); a short etymology; reference chips (frequency, earliest literary use, New Testament quotations).

Read this first: about the English

  • The English is Sir Lancelot Brenton’s translation of 1851. It is careful and literal but uses archaic English (thee/thou, “letted” = hindered, “subtil” = subtle).
  • Brenton translated a different Greek edition (the Vaticanus-based Sixtine text) from the one shown here (Rahlfs 1935). In a hand-checked sample about 1 verse in 7 had some wording difference; usually small, sometimes important — e.g. Ps 39:7, where the Greek shown says “ears” (ὠτία) but Brenton’s English says “a body” (his Greek has σῶμα). Verses where Brenton’s Greek differs noticeably are marked ≠ Greek differs.
  • The two editions also number verses differently in places (Sirach 30–36, Proverbs 24–31, Deuteronomy 29, Epistle of Jeremiah, Exodus 35–40, 1 Esdras, Tobit, 4 Maccabees, Wisdom 17 and others). This edition re-matches the English to the right Greek verse by comparing Brenton’s own Greek text (public domain, eBible grcbrent) with the Greek shown, verse by verse; re-matched verses show Brenton’s number as [Br. 34:1]. Where no reliable match exists the English is hidden rather than showing the wrong verse. Affected chapters carry a ⚠ note at the top.
  • No English is available for Odes, Psalms of Solomon, Tobit (S text), Joshua (A), Judges (A), and the Old Greek Daniel/Susanna/Bel.
  • Always let the Greek decide: the English is a help, not the text.

How to use

Plain meaning. The meaning at the top of the word card comes from a general dictionary of biblical Greek: Dodson’s Greek-English Lexicon (Jeffrey Dodson; public domain, CC0), or, for words Dodson does not have, the brief gloss of STEP Bible TBESG (Tyndale House, CC BY 4.0). Each meaning carries a small label with its source. Other meanings for the word (the LXX lexicon’s gloss, and a “usual LXX sense” chosen automatically to match Brenton’s wording) are listed under More with their labels. Changes made to the dictionary text: spacing tidied; a leading “I”/“to” on verbs and “a/an/the” on nouns removed; Dodson’s “am …” written as “be …”; STEP Bible’s “base: specific” glosses reduced to the base; a long Dodson gloss shortened to at most three of its own senses (the full gloss is under More).

Also in the NT. Up to three New Testament references for the same word, from STEPBible TAGNT (Translators Amalgamated Greek NT, STEPBible.org, CC BY 4.0), words in the NA28 text, matched by dictionary form.

Brenton’s wording. When you tap a Greek word, the card also shows the word Brenton used for it, and highlights it in his verse. You can compare it with the plain meaning, for example ἀόρατος: plain “invisible”, Brenton “unsightly”. Brenton’s translation is not word-by-word, so this match is made automatically. In a fresh hand check it was right 57 times out of 58 when shown. For about 15–17% of words it shows no Brenton word instead of guessing. Articles and other small words are not matched.

Greek or Reader. The switch at the top of a chapter chooses Greek, which shows the Greek text only, large and without English, so you can read it on your own and tap a word when you need help. Reader shows the Greek with the English translation. Your choice is remembered.

Books in two Greek versions. Rahlfs prints two Greek forms of Daniel, Susanna and Bel (the original Old Greek and the later Theodotion version the Church read), Joshua and Judges (the B and A texts), and Tobit (the short BA text and the long Sinaiticus text). Each book appears once in the list. Use the Version switch at the top of the chapter to change versions. Brenton’s English follows Theodotion, B and BA, so it is not shown with the other versions.

  • Tap/click any Greek word: the panel shows the form, its dictionary form (lemma), parsing, gloss, and links. All other forms of the same lemma in the chapter are highlighted.
  • ☰ opens the book list; ‹ › move by chapter (arrow keys on desktop). Your last chapter is remembered on this device.
  • EN shows/hides Brenton's English; ◐ dark/light; Aa text size; ⌕ search by Greek lemma (accents optional), transliteration or English.
  • Psalms use Greek (LXX) numbering: LXX Ps 9–147 are generally one lower than Hebrew/English numbering (LXX Ps 22 = English Ps 23).

Install & offline use

You can install this site as an app: use Install app in the menu, or your browser’s “Add to Home screen” / “Install”. The chapters you read are saved on your device and still open without a connection. To keep the whole Bible offline (all chapters, dictionary and English; about 44 MB), open the menu ☰ → Offline & app → Download everything. The same place shows how much storage is used and lets you remove the offline copy. Nothing is sent anywhere: there is no account, no tracking and no server, and the offline copy stays in your browser’s storage. When a new version of the site is published, the app offers a Reload. Only files that changed are downloaded again.

“≠ Greek differs” badge: on 511 verses, Brenton’s own Greek (eBible grcbrent) is materially different from the Rahlfs Greek shown here. This is an automatic word-overlap comparison that ignores proper names. Where the English plainly does not fit (28 verses), it is hidden. In a hand check of 40 flagged verses, 38 really had English that does not exactly match the Greek shown. About a quarter of those were wording differences; the rest were differences in verse division. The other 2 were false alarms caused by spelling of names and numbers.

Short glosses: the short glosses for the most frequent lemmas were reviewed by hand against STEPBible TBESG (Abbott-Smith) and TFLSJ (LSJ) senses. Modern or odd glosses (e.g. ὀργή “passion; temperament”) were replaced with the usual LXX sense (“anger; wrath”). There were 122 changes; each one, with its old gloss, new gloss and source, is logged in data/regloss_log.csv.

Independent parsing check: no independent Greek morphological analyser (e.g. Morpheus) could be installed offline here. Instead, a random sample of 4,400 noun, adjective and verb tokens was checked against the inflection tables in Wiktionary (via kaikki.org, CC BY-SA 4.0; used only for checking, nothing is copied). Wiktionary covered 3,060 of these tokens. Before corrections, the CATSS tags agreed with Wiktionary on 3,018 (98.6%). Clear tagging errors, where both the form and Wiktionary agree (13 forms, e.g. θώρακα tagged plural), were corrected as overrides. The remaining 28 disagreements are mostly gaps or ambiguities in Wiktionary (Koine -εῖς accusatives, infinitive/optative forms that look the same, two-termination adjectives). These are listed in the source repository.

Known limitations

  • The source text has no punctuation or capitalisation (as in the CCAT data).
  • Glosses are short and are dictionary meanings, not translations of this verse; always check the senses and Logeion/LSJ. The dictionary senses (Abbott-Smith) are written for the New Testament and some LXX-only words have no entry.
  • Morphological tagging comes from the CATSS database. Obvious contradictions were corrected (see above); errors that the ending cannot reveal remain.
  • English re-matching is automatic and checked by sampling, not verse by verse by a human.
